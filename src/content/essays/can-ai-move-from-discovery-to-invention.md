**AI can already discover possibilities that humans have not explored within existing rules. The deeper question is whether it can go further: question those rules, construct new theories, and invent capabilities that did not previously exist.**

## From Discovery to Invention

Go-playing systems uncover unfamiliar moves, while mathematical systems combine established techniques to produce new proof strategies. Yet these achievements still take place within predefined rules. Can AI do more than discover possibilities permitted by a given system? Can it question the rules, redefine a problem, or construct a coherent theory under different premises—and use that theory to create capabilities that did not previously exist?

Discovery means recognizing laws or patterns that already exist but remain unknown to us. Invention means creating new concepts, methods, structures, or tools that allow those laws to be described or used in new ways. The two are related, but not identical.

## 1. AI Does More Than Repeat Its Training Data

Pretraining teaches a language model knowledge and reasoning patterns from large collections of text. Reinforcement learning adjusts its behavioral tendencies through reward signals, making better-performing responses more likely. At first glance, this may look like reranking existing answers.

But the space of sequences a model can generate is vastly larger than its training corpus. It can recombine concepts and methods from different fields to produce solutions never seen in the data. Repeated attempts and feedback can make previously low-probability approaches more stable.

Novelty, however, is not the same as a valid discovery. A never-before-seen answer may be a random combination. It becomes meaningful only when it solves a problem, survives mathematical or experimental testing, and withstands independent scrutiny. “Not present in the training data” does not mean “impossible to derive from what was learned.”

We should distinguish generating something novel, discovering a valid result, and constructing a new theory. The next question is whether AI can move beyond finding new paths within existing rules and begin to change the rules or the way a problem is defined.

## 2. Fixed Rules Do Not Mean All Possibilities Have Been Explored

Go illustrates why fixed rules do not imply that all answers are known. Move 37 in AlphaGo's match against Lee Sedol surprised many professional players. AlphaGo did not change the rules; it revealed a strategic possibility humans had not fully appreciated. AlphaGo Zero went further, developing strong play through self-play without relying on human game records as demonstrations.

A similar pattern has appeared in mathematics. In October 2026, OpenAI reported mathematical papers generated with the assistance of its AI systems. One result established a version of the **quasi-Riemann hypothesis**. This is weaker than the full Riemann hypothesis: in simplified terms, it rules out zeros of the Riemann zeta function and related Dirichlet L-functions in a specified region to the right of real part 7/8.

The proof is notable for connecting mathematical tools. Terence Tao noted that the argument uses an identity linking the Möbius function with Fourier coefficients of certain automorphic forms. Put simply, researchers found a relationship between mathematical objects that are not usually connected in an obvious way, then used it to construct a new proof strategy.

The innovation was not a change to mathematical rules or the creation of new axioms. It was the reorganization of existing tools within an established framework. Like AlphaGo's new strategy, it shows that fixed rules still contain unexplored possibilities.

Still, finding a new route is different from inventing a new means of transportation. Go-playing AI found new moves; mathematical AI found a new proof path. Neither achievement, by itself, changes the basic definition of its domain. We must distinguish searching within a space from changing the space itself.

## 3. From a New Ground Route to the Airplane

Imagine traveling from A to B. If the problem is defined as ground navigation, AI may discover a route no human has taken. But as long as the available modes of movement and the problem definition remain unchanged, it is still searching within the original space.

The airplane changed the boundaries of the problem. It introduced a new vehicle, propulsion system, control variables, and mode of movement. It did not change the laws of nature; aerodynamic principles already existed. Through theory, experimentation, materials, and engineering, humans learned to use those principles and created a new capability.

This illustrates two levels of innovation: finding an unknown solution within a defined space, and expanding or changing the problem space by creating new representations, tools, operations, or theories. The latter changes how natural laws are described and used, rather than violating them.

## 4. Scientific Breakthroughs Begin by Reexamining Foundational Assumptions

Scientific theories are built on definitions, axioms, and assumptions. Those foundations can themselves become objects of inquiry.

Non-Euclidean geometry is a clear example. Euclidean geometry includes the parallel postulate: through a point outside a given line, exactly one line can be drawn parallel to it. Mathematicians asked whether this was the only possible foundation for geometry. By systematically changing the postulate while retaining other basic conditions, they constructed different systems, including hyperbolic and elliptic geometry. These systems have their own rigorous, internally coherent structures.

Einstein's development of relativity likewise involved reexamining classical assumptions. Newtonian mechanics treats space and time as an absolute background. It works remarkably well at everyday speeds and under weak gravitational conditions, but its limits appear in high-speed motion and stronger gravitational settings. Einstein reconsidered the relationships among time, space, motion, and gravity, and developed a more general framework. Relativity did not simply declare Newton's laws wrong; Newtonian mechanics remains a useful approximation in the low-speed, weak-gravity limit.

These examples show that progress can mean questioning the axioms and assumptions on which a theory rests, then constructing a different, coherent explanatory system. Both began with existing knowledge, identified the limits of established frameworks, and reorganized their underlying concepts. Could AI learn to examine the assumptions on which its own models depend and propose a new framework when those assumptions prove inadequate?

## 5. From the Turing Test to Scientific Discovery

The Turing test offers one way to think about intelligence. If people cannot reliably tell whether they are communicating with a human or a machine through text alone, the machine has demonstrated comparable language behavior in that interaction. The test emphasizes externally observable behavior, not whether the machine's internal workings resemble the human brain.

Einstein's elevator thought experiment helps illustrate this reasoning. Imagine a completely enclosed elevator. One is stationary on Earth, inside Earth's gravitational field. Another is far from any significant source of gravity, but a rocket accelerates it upward. If the acceleration is appropriately matched, a person inside either elevator feels the floor pushing against their feet, and objects fall toward the floor in a familiar way. Without seeing outside, the person may be unable to determine through local experiments whether they are standing on Earth or inside an accelerating elevator.

Now let a beam of light enter horizontally through a small window. To an external inertial observer, the light travels in a straight line. But while the light crosses the elevator, the accelerating elevator moves upward. The person inside sees the beam reach the opposite wall at a lower point than where it entered. Relative to the elevator, the path appears to curve downward.

Einstein drew an important inference: if a gravitational elevator and an accelerating elevator are locally indistinguishable, should an observer in a gravitational field also see a similar deflection of light? This reasoning connected gravity, acceleration, and light deflection, becoming an important step in the development of general relativity. The equivalence is local; tidal effects can distinguish the situations over a sufficiently large region.

The elevator experiment and the Turing test are not the same theory, and their notions of indistinguishability differ. The elevator experiment concerns local physical equivalence: under specified conditions, two situations produce the same local effects, allowing an inference from one to be carried over to the other. The Turing test concerns behavior: if conversation alone cannot reliably distinguish a human from a machine, the machine has demonstrated a corresponding level of language ability within that test.

The useful analogy is that judgment need not begin with knowledge of internal mechanisms. It can begin by examining externally observable effects. Could this indistinguishability extend from conversation to scientific research? Imagine placing humans and AI under comparable conditions and giving them an unsolved problem. Could both identify anomalies, formulate hypotheses, design experiments, revise theories in response to evidence, and produce independently verifiable discoveries? Might a higher-level “scientific Turing test” become possible, in which the research process and results no longer reliably reveal whether a discovery came from a human or an AI?

Harari's Oxford lecture, *AI Has Hacked the Code of Human Civilization*, adds another perspective. He argues that large-scale human cooperation depends on institutions such as law, finance, religion, and organizations, which rely heavily on language, writing, records, and rules. Language allows knowledge and experience to accumulate across individuals and generations. AI is increasingly capable of processing these linguistic systems and entering the knowledge systems humanity has built over time.

Scientific knowledge depends on this accumulation. Scientists express theories through language and mathematics, publish them in papers, encode them in programs or formal proofs, and make them available for others to examine, challenge, verify, and extend. AI may therefore participate in the production and evolution of knowledge rather than merely imitate human expression.

Language, however, is not the objective world itself. It can describe physical laws but cannot replace experiments. Mathematics can express a theory but cannot alone prove that it corresponds to reality. To become a scientific discoverer, AI's ability to work with human knowledge must be connected to observation, experimentation, action, and independent verification.

## 6. From Parameter Optimization to Rule Reconstruction

If AI is to develop creative capabilities comparable to those of human scientists, more data, larger models, or reward-based response optimization may not be enough. The object of optimization may need to move beyond model parameters to algorithms, problem definitions, and the assumptions on which theories depend.

This progression can be described in five levels, organized by what the system changes. They represent increasing abstraction, not a fixed sequence of training stages.

**Level 1: Parameter Optimization.** The system adjusts parameters within a fixed model and objective function, such as network weights or hyperparameters. It changes numerical values, not the basic method of solving the problem.

**Level 2: Algorithm and Architecture Innovation.** The system modifies algorithms, network structures, or computational workflows, for example by designing a new optimization algorithm, search strategy, or model architecture.

**Level 3: Objective and Representation Redesign.** The system examines how the problem is defined. Does the objective function represent the outcome we want? Does the data structure omit important relationships? Is there a better representation? It reconsiders what counts as a good answer and which space should be searched.

**Level 4: Assumption and Rule Revision.** The system questions the premises on which a theory depends. Which assumptions are indispensable, and which are inherited conventions? Could changing an axiom or fundamental assumption produce another logically coherent system? The object of exploration has moved from solutions and objectives to the rules themselves.

**Level 5: New Theory Construction.** Under new definitions and assumptions, the system builds a theory that is internally coherent, supports testable predictions, and explains phenomena. It proposes a new framework for understanding the world rather than merely modifying an old model.

Non-Euclidean geometry and relativity illustrate what changing axioms and rebuilding physical theories can make possible. The former shows how changing the parallel postulate can produce new mathematical systems. The latter shows how reconsidering classical assumptions can lead to a broader explanatory framework.

These five levels are not a strictly linear roadmap, nor must higher-level capabilities wait until every lower-level capability is mature. They describe how the objects AI can modify may expand from numerical parameters to methods, objectives, representations, fundamental assumptions, and entire theoretical systems. The deeper question is whether AI can move beyond improving existing solutions to redefining problems and reconstructing the frameworks used to explain them.

## 7. From Problem Solver to Problem Finder, and Then to Theory Builder

The previous section described what AI may learn to change. This section considers the research processes and training mechanisms that could help it develop those capabilities.

A **Problem Solver** is the most common role today: humans define the problem, provide objectives and constraints, and AI searches for a better answer. A **Problem Finder** looks for anomalies that existing theories cannot explain, identifies overlooked variables, and questions whether the original problem was defined appropriately. A **Theory Builder** goes further. It proposes new concepts and assumptions, establishes a coherent structure among them, derives predictions that distinguish its theory from existing ones, and designs experiments to test those predictions.

Moving toward these roles requires changes in training and system architecture. Models cannot rely only on static corpora and one-time reward feedback. They need to accumulate experience over long research processes, generate competing hypotheses, use mathematical tools, simulators, and experimental equipment, design tests that distinguish among hypotheses, and revise their world models in response to results. Reward mechanisms should encourage testable predictions, explanatory power, reproducibility, and informative discoveries—not merely answers that sound convincing.

A possible research loop is to build a model of the current theory, search for phenomena it cannot explain, propose alternative hypotheses, derive their predictions, and design experiments or simulations that distinguish among them. If the evidence does not support the existing theory, the system revises or abandons its assumptions and constructs a new model. That model is tested again and may lead to new questions.

AI would no longer search only within a predefined solution space. It could modify a problem's representation, redefine its objectives, or revise the assumptions of an existing theory. A new theory can change what researchers know to observe and measure, and reveal questions that previously seemed meaningless. The space of scientific exploration can therefore expand as theories themselves change.

The evolution of AI may thus be more than becoming a more efficient Problem Solver. It may gradually acquire the capabilities of a Problem Finder and a Theory Builder—helping identify the limits of existing problems, assumptions, and theories, and participating in the construction of new theoretical and engineering frameworks.

## Conclusion: From Discovering the Known World to Creating New Possibilities

The laws of the objective world exist independently of whether humans or AI recognize them. The history of science shows how humans have revised their understanding of those laws: discovering phenomena that existing theories had not explained, changing fundamental assumptions, building more general frameworks, and turning new understanding into mathematical methods, engineering systems, and practical capabilities.

AI can already explore unknown paths within established rules. The deeper challenge is to develop systems that can examine the objectives, representations, and assumptions on which they rely. When an existing theory cannot explain an observation, can the system propose an alternative? When a problem definition restricts what is possible, can it redefine the problem? When fundamental assumptions prove inadequate, can it construct a new theory and test it against evidence?

This requires AI to move from passive response toward active research: from optimizing existing solutions to proposing hypotheses, and from searching a given space to expanding or reconstructing that space. Genuine invention requires more than generating novel ideas. It also requires designing experiments, accepting counterevidence, revising models, and turning new theories into capabilities that can be tested and realized.

An open question therefore remains. If humans can build on existing knowledge to challenge established theories, create new concepts, and discover previously unknown laws, can AI gradually develop the same capacity? Might it one day do more than find a route no human has taken? Could it invent a new means of transportation—or even redefine what a “road” can be?

**True invention does not mean creating out of nothing or escaping the objective world. It means discovering possibilities that were not previously understood and creating new ways to realize them. Whether AI can reach this point will depend on its ability to move beyond learning existing knowledge and become a system that can actively question, reconstruct, and create knowledge.**
